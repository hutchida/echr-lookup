# S2: Parse + chunk

## 1. What this is, in plain English

The corpus is stored as long documents: a judgment is a web page of 100–300 numbered paragraphs, the Convention is
a text file, the Human Rights Act is an XML file. A RAG system (Retrieval-Augmented Generation: find relevant
passages first, then let a language model write an answer *from those passages only*) cannot work with a whole
document. It needs **small passages it can find, quote and cite**.

S2 does two jobs. **Parsing** turns each stored file into a list of *paragraphs*. Each paragraph has a stable ID such
as `001-57619§88` (Soering, paragraph 88), a section label (Procedure / The Facts / The Law / Operative provisions /
Other) and its text. Those IDs are what every later citation points at, so they must be the same on every run.
**Chunking** then cuts the paragraphs into *chunks*: the passages that get embedded (turned into a list of numbers
that captures meaning) and searched. A chunk is usually a few paragraphs long, and always remembers exactly which
paragraphs it contains, so a search hit can be turned back into a citation.

There are three chunking strategies, because nobody knows in advance which one retrieves best, and S6's eval will
measure it: **fixed** (a sliding window of 256 tokens, blind to paragraph borders), **recursive** (whole
paragraphs packed up to 256 tokens) and **by_section** (like recursive, up to 480 tokens, never crossing from, say,
the Facts into the Law). A *token* is the unit a language model reads; it is roughly a word or a piece of a word
("deportation" is one token, "ill-founded" is three).

A third job is a bonus that the stats question needs: for decisions the Court declared inadmissible, HUDOC's
metadata almost never says *why*. S2 reads the decision's text and extracts the grounds ("manifestly ill-founded",
"non-exhaustion of domestic remedies", …), so "what were the most common reasons?" can be answered with SQL.

## 2. Why it matters for answer quality

Take the question "Does the Convention stop the UK deporting criminals?". The strongest sentence in the whole
corpus is the one in Soering §88: *"Article 3 (art. 3) makes no provision for exceptions…"*. Three things have to go
right for the answer to cite it:

- the paragraph must be **numbered correctly**. If the parser mis-numbers one paragraph, every later citation in
  that judgment is off by one, and the citation checker (S4) will either reject a true quote or approve a false one;
- the paragraph must be **intact**. In Soering, §111's key sentence sits in an *unnumbered* block after the number.
  A naive parser that only keeps lines starting with a number loses it;
- the **chunk** around it must be a good unit. §88 is long (3 chunks under `recursive`): a `fixed` window may start
  in §87 and end in §89 and so dilute it, while `by_section` bundles it with the rest of the Law section (up to
  480 tokens). Which is best for retrieval is exactly what the eval will say.

## 3. How it works here

```
stored file ──parse_document──► {"document", "paragraphs"} ──chunk_paragraphs(strategy)──► chunks
(.html/.xml/.txt)               paragraph ids: <doc>§88 §op2 §u7 §art8     ids: <doc>:<strategy>:<n>
                                section labels, grounds from text           paragraph_ids: [..], token_count
```

Everything lives in `py/rag/parse/`:

| File | What it does |
|---|---|
| `__init__.py` | `parse_document(entry, raw)` dispatches on the stored format and fills in the grounds. Re-exports `STRATEGIES`, `chunk_paragraphs`. |
| `hudoc.py` | HUDOC HTML → paragraphs. One pass over the `<p>`/`<li>` blocks with a small state machine (cover page → headings → numbered text → operative provisions → signatures → separate opinions). |
| `legislation.py` | The Convention text (one paragraph per Article, `art8`, protocols `p1-art1`) and the HRA XML (one per section: `s3`, `s7A`; Schedule 1 `sch1-art8`). |
| `grounds.py` | Inadmissibility grounds from text (sentence rules, see below). |
| `chunking.py` | The three strategies. |
| `tokens.py` | `approx_count_tokens`: the cheap counter (words + punctuation × 1.25) used when no real tokenizer is passed. |
| `__main__.py` | The command-line try-it tool. |
| `tests/` | 45 tests (2 more need the `ml` group): snapshot checks, unit tests for the chunkers, and the G0b grounds oracle (`grounds_oracle.json`). |

**Paragraph IDs.** `§88` numbered paragraph; `§op2` the 2nd item of "For these reasons…"; `§u7` the 7th unnumbered
paragraph (decisions and legal summaries without numbers, and the separate opinions of judges, whose numbering
restarts at 1); `echr-convention§art8`; `ukpga-1998-42§s3`.

**What the HUDOC parser knows (all seen in the snapshot):**

- a numbered paragraph continues in following unnumbered blocks (quotes, lists): they are attached, joined with a
  newline (Soering §111);
- an old document may glue paragraph N+1 onto the end of N in one block, or omit the full stop after the number
  (McCann §41/§42, §131): the parser expects the strict sequence 1, 2, 3, … and splits on it;
- a quoted judgment keeps its own numbers inside a paragraph ("74. It would be quite wrong…" inside para 69 of
  *Times Newspapers*). A number is only accepted if it is the next one, or (with a full stop) a small skip with no
  proper "next" later in the document;
- the operative part restarts numbering: items become `§op1…`; modern judgments list them as `<li>` or `(a)`;
- separate opinions and appendices (after "Done in English…" and the signatures) become `§u<n>` in section `Other`;
- 14 decisions and all legal summaries have no numbers: every paragraph of prose gets `§u<n>`, the cover page
  (composition of the Chamber, "Having regard to…") is dropped. Legal summaries switch to The Facts / The Law at the
  "Facts:" and "Law:" lines;
- **a documented exception:** in *Joseph Grant v UK* (`001-90402`) HUDOC's HTML lost the printed number of 18
  paragraphs (17–19, 23–29, 31–36, 38–39). The text is kept as unnumbered `§u` paragraphs in the right order,
  and the test lists those 18 numbers as the only allowed gap in the whole snapshot.

**Chunk details.**

- `fixed`: the document is a stream of words; each window takes words until the token budget is full, the next
  window starts so that 32 tokens overlap, and `paragraph_ids` lists every paragraph the window touches.
- `recursive`: whole paragraphs are packed until the next one would overflow. A paragraph that is itself too long is
  split on sentences, and a sentence that is too long on words (a last-resort hard cut for a single huge "word").
- `by_section`: the same, run separately on each run of paragraphs sharing a section, limit 480 (the `max_tokens`
  argument does not change this; bge-small reads at most 512 tokens).
- `count_tokens` is any `text -> int`. The code subtracts what the counter charges for the empty string (the
  `[CLS]`/`[SEP]` tokens of a real tokenizer), so the real embedder's `count_tokens` can be passed unchanged.
  `token_count` is always re-counted on the final chunk text.

**The cheap counter.** With the real bge-small tokenizer, the snapshot's paragraphs have 1.05 tokens per
"word or punctuation mark" on average (99th percentile 1.18, worst paragraph 1.48). `approx_count_tokens` multiplies
by 1.25 and rounds up, so it *over*-counts: under it the mean chunk is about 171–179 tokens (`fixed`, `recursive`)
instead of 256, and nothing exceeds the real limit (tested). With a real tokenizer chunks are close to the full 256.

**Inadmissibility grounds** (`grounds.py`). Split the whole text into sentences; ignore sentences that recite the
Convention or the Rules, report a party's argument ("The Government submitted that…"), negate the ground ("cannot
be considered manifestly ill-founded") or leave the question open ("need not decide whether…"); in the rest, a
ground counts when its phrase appears in a sentence where the Court is rejecting something. "Rejected under Article
35 § 1" is told apart (six/four months vs. exhaustion of remedies) from the three sentences before. This only
runs for every *decision* whose outcome is `inadmissible` / `partly_inadmissible`, whether or not HUDOC's metadata
already names a ground. If nothing is found and there is no metadata, the grounds stay `null` (honest: "not
extracted") rather than a guess; that does not happen anywhere in the snapshot.

*Metadata plus text (the union rule).* HUDOC sometimes names only some of the grounds: K2 v. the UK is tagged
"(Art. 35-3-a) Manifestly ill-founded", but the decision also rejects the Article 14 complaint for failure to
exhaust domestic remedies. So the stored set is the **union** of the metadata grounds and the grounds the text
states, in enum order. The extractor is the same conservative one as above (the Court's own reasoning or operative
part rejecting a complaint, not a mention or quotation). `inadmissibility_grounds_source` is `"metadata"` when the
stored set is exactly what the metadata said (the text adds nothing, or the metadata was not changed), and `"text"`
as soon as the text adds at least one ground (or the metadata had none), because then the stored set is no longer
only what HUDOC said. In the snapshot only two decisions carry metadata grounds: K2 (`001-172143`) gains
`non_exhaustion` and becomes `"text"`; `001-182755` (`non_exhaustion`) is unchanged and stays `"metadata"`.

**Agreement with G0b's oracle: 35 of 35 decisions (100%) exactly match, and the test prints the rate.** The check runs `parse_document` itself over all 35 oracle entries, including the two whose manifest already names a ground (K2's stored set is now the union above). I
reused G0b's prototype method, so agreement was expected; the more useful result is the review I did on top:

- *Disagreements about the answer:* none. The final sets are identical for all 35.
- *Disagreements about evidence:* the prototype's chosen evidence sentence was wrong (even though the set was right)
  for two decisions. **K2** (`001-172143`, `non_exhaustion`): the sentence it matched says the Court "does not need
  to reach any firm conclusion on whether the applicant has exhausted domestic remedies", i.e. the opposite;
  but a different sentence ("the Article 14 complaint … must be rejected … for failure to exhaust domestic
  remedies") does support it, so the oracle is right. **Times Newspapers** (`001-188457`, `non_exhaustion`): the
  matched sentence only frames the question; the concluding sentence ("the present application must be declared
  inadmissible as the applicants have failed to exhaust domestic remedies") confirms it. I added "whether" and
  "need not" to the skip list so the first kind of sentence no longer counts, and the answers stay the same.
- *Added:* "four months" counts as `out_of_time` (the rule since February 2022; no snapshot decision needs it).
- *Not independently checked:* I read the evidence sentences of all non-"manifestly ill-founded" grounds (12 of
  them) and the multi-ground cases, not all 35 line by line. Neither I nor the oracle's author is a lawyer; and
  both only report grounds the Court states in a concluding sentence. A decision rejecting different complaints on
  different grounds can have a ground we do not see if it is phrased unusually.

## 4. Try it

Run these from the repo root. The CLI is `cd py && uv run python -m rag.parse <item id or file> [--summary] [--para N]
[--chunks fixed|recursive|by_section] [--max-tokens N] [--real-tokens]`. (`--real-tokens` uses the bge-small tokenizer
and needs `uv sync --group ml`.)

1. **Soering's key sentence.**
   `cd py && uv run python -m rag.parse 001-57619 --para 88`
   → `001-57619§88 [The Law]` followed by "Article 3 (art. 3) makes no provision for exceptions and no derogation from
   it is permissible…" and, below it, the continuation "The question remains whether the extradition of a fugitive…"
   → proves the citation target exists with the exact ID the answer will cite, and that unnumbered continuations are
   kept inside their paragraph.
   Then `--para 111`: the paragraph ends with "…This finding in no way puts in question the good faith of the United
   Kingdom Government…", the unnumbered tail the naive parser loses.

2. **Sections and numbering at a glance.**
   `uv run python -m rag.parse 001-57619 --summary`
   → a table: Procedure §1..§10, The Facts §11..§79, The Law §80..§128, Operative provisions §op1..§op6, Other
   (the concurring opinion) §u3..§u35, and `{'numbered': 128, …}` → proves numbers run 1…128 with none missing and that
   the operative part and the judge's opinion are kept apart from the numbered text.
   Now try `uv run python -m rag.parse 001-90402 --summary`: Joseph Grant shows `numbered: 22` out of 40, because
   HUDOC's file lost the numbers of 18 paragraphs → shows why the tests carry an explicit "known gap" list.

3. **A decision with no numbers, and its grounds.**
   `uv run python -m rag.parse 001-127061 --summary`
   → "inadmissibility grounds (text): non_exhaustion", and a decision such as `001-98645` (Miah) prints
   "manifestly_ill_founded, incompatible_ratione_materiae". Then `uv run python -m rag.parse 001-4596 --para u1`
   → an unnumbered decision cites by running index (`001-4596§u1`) → proves the "why was it inadmissible?" stats
   question has data to work with, and that numberless documents are still citable.

4. **See where each strategy cuts Soering §88.**
   `uv run python -m rag.parse 001-57619 --chunks recursive | grep '§88'` → three chunks, `recursive:101`–`103`, all
   `§88` only (the long paragraph was split on sentences);
   `uv run python -m rag.parse 001-57619 --chunks fixed | grep '§88'` → chunks `fixed:113`–`116` that start and end mid-
   paragraph, some listing two paragraphs (`§87..§88`, `§88..§89`);
   `uv run python -m rag.parse 001-57619 --chunks by_section | head` → 89 chunks of ~350–480 tokens, each inside one
   section → proves the three strategies really differ in how much of a paragraph's neighbourhood a chunk carries.
   That difference is what the eval (S6) will turn into numbers.

5. **The Convention and the Human Rights Act are citable too.**
   `uv run python -m rag.parse echr-convention --para art8` → "Article 8 – Right to respect for private and family
   life", with "1. Everyone has the right…" and "2. There shall be no interference…" on separate lines;
   `uv run python -m rag.parse ukpga-1998-42 --para s3` → "Human Rights Act 1998, section 3 – Interpretation of
   legislation (1) So far as it is possible to do so…" → proves a question about section 3 HRA can cite
   `ukpga-1998-42§s3`. (Protocol articles: `--para p1-art1`; Schedule 1 of the HRA: `--para sch1-art8`.)

6. **Run the proofs yourself.**
   `cd py && uv run pytest rag/parse -q -s` → 41 tests pass in a few seconds and the output includes
   "grounds oracle agreement: 35/35 = 100%" and "parse + 3 chunkers over 98 documents (approximate counter): ~2s" →
   this is the budget check (limit 15 s). With the real tokenizer (`uv run --group ml pytest rag/parse -q -s`) it adds
   "real bge-small tokenizer: … in ~17s; largest chunk per strategy: {'fixed': 256, 'recursive': 256, 'by_section':
   480}" (limit 45 s).

## 5. Limits / what can go wrong

- **Numbering is inferred, not read.** The parser trusts the "next number" rule. A source where numbers are lost
  (Joseph Grant) gets unnumbered `§u` paragraphs in the middle of numbered ones; a citation to those is stable but
  not the number printed in the official judgment. Anything that would silently drop or duplicate numbers fails the
  tests, but a *new* document from the full corpus can still hit a pattern not in the 96 snapshot files.
- **Short fragments are dropped, on purpose.** An unnumbered block shorter than 60 characters between numbered
  paragraphs (a list of counsel's names, a stray line) is discarded unless it continues the previous paragraph.
  The cover page, signatures and footers are dropped too. "Done in English…" starts the signature block, which is
  skipped until a separate opinion or appendix begins.
- **Sub-headings are removed from the text** (they are not paragraphs); a paragraph that really is short, has no final
  punctuation and starts a new unnumbered block can be mistaken for one.
- **The Convention PDF is plain text.** Article titles are found by a rule (short line, no final punctuation, not a
  continuation); Protocol 16's articles are out of order in the PDF and are re-sorted. The Convention's text is the
  consolidated current version and the HRA XML is the current version (with amendment text inlined, no history).
  One paragraph per Article means very long Articles (35, 6) are one citation target each; `recursive` and
  `by_section` still cut them into chunks.
- **Grounds are best-effort.** Only the grounds the Court states in a concluding sentence are found, and only in
  English. Partly inadmissible decisions get the grounds of their inadmissible *parts*, not a per-complaint
  breakdown. A decision where nothing matches would keep `null` grounds.
- **Chunk sizes are not equal under the cheap counter** (it over-counts, so chunks are smaller than 256 "real"
  tokens). When S3 passes the embedder's own `count_tokens`, expect close to 256 and 480.
- **`by_section` ignores `max_tokens`**; its 480 limit is a constant (`BY_SECTION_MAX_TOKENS`).
- **Chunk indexes are 0-based** and depend on the document's other paragraphs: re-parsing a changed document
  renumbers its chunks (which is fine, S3 replaces all chunks of a document at once).
