# S4: Answer + citations

## 1. What this is, in plain English

S4 turns a question into an answer you can check. There are two ways it can answer.

**Reading path (RAG).** RAG means *retrieval-augmented generation*: first *retrieve* the most relevant paragraphs of case law from our index (that is S3's search), then ask a language model (the *generator*) to write an answer using **only** those paragraphs. We ask the model for a list of short *claims*, each tagged with the IDs of the paragraphs that back it (an ID looks like `001-57619§88`: document, then paragraph). Our code, not the model, assembles the final text and puts markers like `[Soering v UK §88]` after each sentence. Each citation also carries a `doc_type` (judgment, decision, communicated case, legal summary or legislation), because a legal summary is the Court's staff describing a judgment and must never be mistaken for the judgment itself. Its `quote` is chosen by `best_quote`: a verbatim run of whole sentences (see section 3).

**Answer first.** For ordinary "what does the law say / what has the Court done" questions the model writes a **short answer** first: one plain sentence that answers the question directly ("Not as a general rule: …"), citing 1 to 3 paragraphs that *together* support it. Then come 3 to 6 **points** (evidence, each about one case), grouped under short neutral headings (`group`) such as "Where the Court found a breach" and "Where the Court allowed the State's action". In the data these are two optional fields on each claim: `role` (`short_answer` or `point`) and `group`. Giving a direct answer about *what the law does* is neutral; what the system still never does is say whether a law or judgment is good or bad, or whether the UK should leave the Convention. Questions that ask for that ("Should the UK leave the ECHR?") keep the older shape: a one-sentence notice and the strongest sourced points on each side, with no verdict.

**Counting path (stats).** A question like "how many … were declared inadmissible?" is not something a model should *estimate* from a few paragraphs. It is a counting job, so we count rows in the database with SQL, and show you the SQL. The model never writes SQL: it only fills in a few parameters (which article, which outcome, which topic) and our code slots them into a fixed, parameterised template.

Three honesty rules shape how a count is reported:

- **Both numbers.** "How many Grand Chamber judgments are there, and how many of them found a violation?" asks for a total *and* a subset. The code runs the same template twice (once without the outcome filter, once with it) and says "The indexed corpus has 10 Grand Chamber judgments; 9 of them found at least one violation." The total is returned as `scope_total` (it is `null` for ordinary questions).
- **Every filter in plain words.** The sentence names each filter that is in the SQL ("Grand Chamber judgments", "raising Article 3", "that were declared inadmissible"), and none that is not. It never says "UK" as if it were a filter: every document in the corpus is a UK case, so the SQL has no filter on the respondent State, and the explanation says so.
- **Examples illustrate, they do not prove.** Under the count come up to 8 *example* cases (`matching_cases`). When the count is about violations, an example quotes the operative clause that states a violation ("Holds that there has been a violation of Article 3 …"); if no clause of that case does, the case is listed with **no quote** (`paragraph_id` is `null`) rather than with a quote that contradicts the count. The count's claims cite no paragraph at all: the SQL is their evidence.

A small **router** picks the path. Obvious counting words ("how many", "number of", "most common", "percentage") go straight to the stats path with no model call; vague ones ("how often", "trend") are sent to a cheap model (Haiku) to decide; everything else reads sources.

Then comes the part that makes the answers trustworthy: **per-claim verification**. A second, cheap model reads *only the paragraph(s) one claim cites* and says `supported` or `unsupported`. Unsupported claims are deleted from the answer text (but kept in `claims[]` so the UI can show what was dropped). If the verifier itself fails, the claim is kept and labelled `unverified`, never silently trusted. The short answer is checked like any other claim, but against *all* its cited paragraphs together (it may summarise them; it must not add anything they do not support). **Reach of the holding.** The short answer's first words must match how far the cited paragraphs reach. If they go both ways: "Not as a general rule: …" or "It depends on …". If a cited paragraph states a general rule that answers the question as asked, lead with that rule. If the paragraphs only decide particular applicants or circumstances while the question is general, begin with the holding itself ("In *X v UK* the Court held that these applicants …"). A bare "Yes" or "No" (in any form: "Yes, where …", "Yes, in some circumstances") is not allowed unless a cited paragraph states that answer in general terms. This is a prompt rule, checked in the tests by looking at the prompt text; its effect on real answers is only measured by the (paid) judged eval.

If it is rejected, the pipeline asks the cheap model once or twice to rewrite it from the points that *did* pass (the model picks which points to summarise, and the new sentence cites exactly those), and verifies the rewrite the same way; if that also fails, the short answer is shown as "left out" like any other unsupported claim.

## 2. Why it matters for answer quality

Models write fluent text whether or not the sources say it. In a live test of "Does the Convention stop the UK deporting criminals?" against real snapshot paragraphs, the generator produced the claim *"In Ndidi v UK … the Court found no violation, so the Convention did not bar his deportation there"* and cited §74 and §21. Those paragraphs only frame the question and describe the domestic decision; they do not contain the Court's conclusion. The verifier read them and returned `unsupported` ("they do not state that the …"), so the sentence was removed. Without this step a reader would have clicked a citation, found nothing, and stopped trusting every other citation too.

The same idea applies to counting: "27 inadmissible deportation complaints" is only credible if you can see the SQL, and the definition of "deportation" (which HUDOC metadata does not have) is spelled out as a text match.

Honesty about scope matters as much. Asked "How many Grand Chamber judgments are there, and how many of them found a violation?", an earlier version gave one number and listed example quotes such as "Holds … that there has been no violation" under a count of violations. Now the answer reads "10 Grand Chamber judgments; 9 of them found at least one violation", and the one example without a violation clause is listed with no quote. Likewise, a short answer that opened "Yes, where …" after cited paragraphs about two particular groups of detainees would have suggested a general rule the Court never stated; the reach-of-holding rule forces the answer to name whom the holding covered.

## 3. How it works here

```
question
   |
 router ──────── "how many / most common / percentage …" ──► STATS
   | (everything else; vague cases ask Haiku)                   |
   v                                                            v
 RAG: rag.search (hybrid + rerank, k=12)  +          Haiku extracts parameters
     Haiku-planned queries (legal test, breach,       (validated against fixed lists)
     no breach), searched in parallel with it          -> SQL template -> read-only txn,
   v                                                      3 s timeout -> rows + count (+ scope_total)
                                                      + example cases (operative clause or no quote)
 context = paragraphs, spread over cases (max 4 per case),    |
   recited statute text last, plus operative provisions       |
   [001-57619§88] Soering v. UK (judgment, 1989…)             |
   v                                                            |
 Sonnet writes JSON: points (with group) + short_answer        |
   v                                                            |
 unknown IDs repaired or rejected                               |
   v                                                            |
 Haiku checks each claim vs its paragraphs (parallel threads)   |
   v                                                            v
 unsupported claims removed ──────────► Answer (steps, cost, models, < 50 KB)
```

Files in `py/rag/answer/`:

| File | What it does |
|---|---|
| `llm.py` | `complete()`: the **only** Anthropic call site. Explicit client (`rag.config` key + base URL, never the shell's env), retry on 429/5xx with tenacity, thinking switched off (it eats the token budget and adds seconds), prompt caching for long system prompts, every call logged via `rag.index.log_cost`. |
| `llm_fake.py` | `LLM_MODE=fake`: deterministic canned outputs so the whole pipeline runs offline. |
| `router.py` | `route()`: heuristics first, Haiku only when ambiguous. |
| `plan.py` | Detects value questions. A small model turns *every* reading question into 3 to 5 neutral search queries (3 or 4 for non-value questions): for value questions the Court's reasoning and each side's position; for other questions the legal test, when the Court found a breach, and when it did not. |
| `context.py` | Runs `rag.search` (plus the planned queries), loads the cited paragraphs from the `paragraphs` table, **diversifies** them (at most 4 paragraphs per case; paragraphs that merely recite domestic legislation go behind the Court's own reasoning), adds each retrieved case's *operative provisions* ("Holds that there has been no violation of Article 8", skipping boilerplate like "that the State is to pay …") so outcome claims have a paragraph to cite, builds the labelled context. |
| `generate.py` | The neutral system prompt, the "answer first" and value-question rules, JSON claims (short answer, points, groups), citation-ID repair/rejection, the short-answer rewrite. |
| `verify.py` | Per-claim verification with Haiku in a thread pool (700 tokens for the reply, one retry if the reply is cut off or not JSON). |
| `stats.py` | Parameter extraction, SQL templates, the read-only executor and SQL validator, the `StatsQueryResult`. `wants_total_and_subset()` decides when to also run the unfiltered total (`count_of: "total_and_subset"`, or "how many …, and how many …" in the question); `_scope_phrase()` words every filter; `pick_operative_clause()` and `_matching_cases()` choose the example quote (an operative clause stating the counted outcome, else no quote). |
| `pipeline.py` | `answer()`: assembles the `Answer` (text from claims: short answer, then each group's heading and points; citations with `doc_type` and verbatim quotes, steps, cost), the short-answer repair, size caps. For a stats answer, `stats_citations()` lists the quoted example paragraphs as `citations`; no claim cites them. |
| `citations.py` | Marker names (`[Soering v UK §88]`), `link_target` (`/case/001-57619#p88`), and `best_quote()`: splits the paragraph into sentences (a full stop after "v.", "art.", "no." does not end one; `;` and `:` never do; a line break does), scores each sentence by the claim's distinctive words (numbers such as an Article count most), and returns the best run of whole sentences that fits in 300 characters and contains the best sentence. Only when that one sentence is longer than 300 characters is it cut, at a clause boundary if possible; the web page then adds "…" where the quote starts or stops mid-sentence. Always an exact substring. |
| `__main__.py` | CLI, `pnpm ask "<question>"`. |

Two safety layers on the SQL path: our validator allows only one `SELECT`/`WITH` over `documents`, `paragraphs`, `chunks` (no writes, no other tables, no `pg_*`); and even if it were bypassed, the query runs inside a `READ ONLY` transaction that is always rolled back, with `statement_timeout = 3 s`.

## 4. Try it

Tries 1, 2, 3 and 6 work offline. The rest need the snapshot ingested (`pnpm ingest:snapshot`) and, for real answers, `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` in `.env`.

1. **Citations are checked (offline).** Run `cd py && uv run pytest rag/answer -q -k "unsupported or invented or rejected"` → the tests pass → they show that an invented paragraph ID is rejected, and that a claim the verifier marks unsupported disappears from `answer_text` but stays in `claims[]`.
2. **The SQL cannot write (offline).** Run `cd py && uv run pytest rag/answer/tests/test_answer_stats.py -q -k "write or validator or timeout"` → pass → `DELETE`, `DROP`, a second statement, other tables and `pg_sleep` are refused by the validator, a `DELETE` that sneaks past it still fails with "read-only transaction", and a slow query is cancelled after the timeout.
3. **Counting goes to SQL, and reports both numbers (offline).** Run `LLM_MODE=fake pnpm run ask "How many Grand Chamber judgments are in the corpus, and how many of them found a violation?"` → route `stats`; the ANSWER is "The indexed corpus has 10 Grand Chamber judgments; 9 of them found at least one violation.", the SQL block has two numbered queries ("1) the total, without the outcome filter", "2) how many of those have the outcome"), and CITATIONS lists example operative clauses ("Holds … that there has been a violation of Article 3 …") → shows that a two-part question gets both numbers, that the filters are in plain words, and that examples quote the clause that states the counted outcome. (Offline tests: `cd py && uv run pytest rag/answer/tests/test_answer_stats.py -q -k "total or subset or clause or example"`.)
4. **Counting goes to SQL.** Run `pnpm run ask "How many UK deportation complaints in the corpus were declared inadmissible, and the most common reasons?"` → route `stats`, a count, a table of reasons, the SQL (look for `p.text ~* '[[:<:]](deport|expel|…'` and `>= 3`) and a list of example decisions → the topic "deportation" is defined by a visible text match, and every number can be reproduced by pasting the SQL into `psql`. Now run the same question with `--route rag` to see why counting is a bad fit for reading: you get a few cited examples, not a total.
5. **Answer first, then balanced evidence.** Run `pnpm run ask "Does the Convention stop the UK deporting criminals?"` → route `rag`; the ANSWER starts with a one-sentence direct answer ("Not as a general rule: …") with its markers, then grouped points (for example "Where the Court found a breach" and "Where the Court allowed the State's action"), covering Article 3 and Article 8, each with `[Case v UK §n]` markers; in CLAIMS the first line is marked `(short answer)` and every line shows its `[group]` and a ✓ badge → shows that the system states the bottom line of what the Court has done, and then proves it with cases on each side. See `docs/learn/S4-demo.md` for a full transcript. Open one `link_target` such as `/case/001-57619#p88` in Gate 2's case viewer later to land on the highlighted paragraph.
6. **Quote windows (offline).** Run `cd py && uv run pytest rag/answer/tests/test_answer_citations.py -q` → pass → a quote is verbatim, never opens on "and …" after a semicolon, does not end just before the sentence that names the Article, does not split at "v." or "art. 3", and an over-long single sentence is cut inside the 300-character budget. Reach of holding: `cd py && uv run pytest rag/answer/tests/test_answer_first.py -q -k reach` → pass → the prompt forbids a bare "Yes"/"No" opening unless a cited paragraph states the general rule. (Whether real answers obey it is only measured by the paid, judged eval.)
7. **Neutrality.** Run `pnpm run ask "Should the UK leave the ECHR?"` → one sentence says it is a value question and gives no verdict, then cited claims on each side (Convention Article 58 on denunciation, cases where the Court found against the UK and where it upheld it, dissents), with no recommendation → shows that the question is answered neutrally rather than refused. Compare with the stats questions: `pnpm run ask "How many UK judgments found at least one violation?"` explains that "at least one violation" means outcomes `violation` plus `mixed`. (Live version: `cd py && RUN_LIVE=1 uv run pytest rag/answer/tests/test_answer_live.py -s`, costs a few cents.)
8. **See the cost.** Run `pnpm run ask "How has Article 8 been applied in UK immigration cases?"` and read the STEPS block at the bottom → one line per step with tokens, dollars and milliseconds; the generator (Sonnet) dominates cost and time, the per-claim verifier (Haiku, run in parallel) is cheap → shows why the router and verifier use the small model.

## 5. Limits / what can go wrong

- **The verifier is a model too.** It catches overreach well, but it can wrongly reject a fair claim or pass a subtle one. `unverified` is shown when it fails; there is no human in the loop. Its reply used to be cut off at 200 tokens for claims that cite several paragraphs, which showed up as "Unverified: Verification failed (JSONDecodeError)"; it now has 700 tokens and one retry (a test feeds it a truncated first reply).
- **Verification only checks claim vs. cited paragraph.** It does not check that the answer is complete, or that retrieval found the best paragraphs. If search misses the key case, the answer will be well cited and incomplete.
- **"About deportation" is a definition, not metadata.** HUDOC has no topic field, so the stats path defines it in SQL and says so in every answer: the document raises Article 3, 8 or 5(1)(f) (the provisions that govern removal and detention pending deportation) **and** at least 2 of its paragraphs contain a word starting `deport`, `expel`, `expulsion`, `extradit` or `removal`. The article test removes false positives such as *Field and Others v UK* ("expulsion from the House of Lords", Articles 14/P1-3); the paragraph count removes passing mentions. It still differs from the hand-curated list in the snapshot README (27 decisions): the definition finds 27 as well, but swaps *K2 v UK* (one matching paragraph) for *Z. and T. v UK*. Other counting definitions are stated the same way: "declared inadmissible" means the *whole* application (outcome `inadmissible`; the number of partly inadmissible matches is reported next to it), "at least one violation" means outcome `violation` or `mixed`, and "Grand Chamber" is HUDOC's chamber field.
- **Inadmissibility reasons depend on S2.** HUDOC names a ground in only a few decisions; the rest come from S2's text extraction, which can miss one: *K2 v UK* also rejects a complaint for non-exhaustion (para 70); S2-fix (#14) now stores `non_exhaustion` for it (the union of metadata and text grounds), but any other decision whose text rejects a ground the extractor misses would still be one short. Decisions without a ground are shown as "(not stated)".
- **Example quotes are illustrations, and may be missing.** A matching case with no operative clause stating the counted outcome (for example a decision, or an old judgment whose operative section is not split into clauses) is listed without a quote. For counts that are not about violations, the example paragraph is still the best topical match, not proof of the filter. The count's claims cite nothing: the SQL is the only evidence for the number.
- **"Both numbers" is triggered by wording.** The total-and-subset path runs when the parameter extractor returns `total_and_subset` or the question matches "how many …, and how many (of them) …"; a differently phrased two-part question gets only one number.
- **Quote windows are heuristic.** Sentence splitting uses a short abbreviation list (`v.`, `art.`, `no.` …); an unusual abbreviation can still split a sentence, and a sentence longer than 300 characters is cut (shown with "…").
- **Reach of the holding is a prompt rule, not a check.** No code verifies the opening words of a short answer; the verifier only checks that the cited paragraphs support it. The rule makes a wrongly general "Yes" less likely, not impossible.
- **The stats path only knows its templates.** It can count and break down by reason, article, outcome, year or document type, filtered by article, outcome, document type, chamber, grounds, dates and keywords, and can return the size of the top group ("the most common ground and how many decisions rest on it"). Other kinds of question are not supported; an unbuildable query falls back to the reading path.
- **A short answer is a summary, and summaries are easy to over-claim.** In live tests the verifier often rejected the first short answer (it named a case it did not cite, or generalised: "in many cases"). The prompt now asks for the points first and the short answer last, naming only cases it cites, and the rewrite step catches most of the rest; it is still not guaranteed: in the last four Q1 asks of the build one short answer ended up shown as "left out" (the points still carried the answer). The short answer reports what the Court held in the cited cases; it is not a complete statement of the law, so read it with the points under it.
- **The eval's citation accuracy drops because of the short answer.** The judge scores *each* cited paragraph on whether it supports the claim "on its own". A short answer cites 1 to 3 paragraphs that support it *together*, so some of its pairs always score false. On 3 judged answers (Q1 twice, Q2) the 18 point-claim citations all scored true (1.00) while the short answers' 10 citations scored 6 (0.60). That is why recursive hybrid_rerank citation accuracy went from 0.98 to 0.93 (see `docs/eval-results.md`).
- **Better evidence costs a little.** Every reading question now makes one extra cheap model call (the query planner, ~$0.0002, about 1.5 s, run while the first search runs) and the generator sees ~40–50 paragraphs instead of ~35, so an answer costs about $0.025 and takes 8 to 12 s warm (15 s is the budget; a rewrite adds about 3 s when it is needed).
- **Value questions get positions, not a verdict, and that costs time.** "Should …?", "Is it right …?", "Is X good for the UK?" first go through a cheap Haiku call that writes neutral search queries (the Court's reasoning, the State's side, the individual's side, the legislation); each is searched and the results are interleaved. The answer prompt then requires the Court's reasoning and a cited, attributed position for each side, never a refusal and never a verdict. This adds about 3–4 s (value questions take ~10–11 s warm). If the sources hold no explicit arguments for and against (e.g. "Is the Human Rights Act good for the UK?"), the answer sets out what the Act empowers courts to do against what it leaves to Parliament; a judge that wants arguments *for and against the Act itself* may still mark that kind of answer as not balanced, because the corpus contains none.
- **Prompt caching only helps for long prompts** (about 1,000+ tokens); the system prompts are shorter, so no cache hits occurred (tokens_cached = 0 in the demos).
- **Latency budget.** Warm, a reading answer takes about 8 to 11 s (Sonnet generation 4 to 7 s, verification 1 to 4 s, rerank ~1 s, planning overlapped with the first search) and a counting answer about 1.5 s; the budget is 15 s, and Anthropic API latency varies from minute to minute, so an occasional slow answer is possible. The embedder and reranker must be loaded once per process (the API does that at startup; the CLI does it before starting the clock), otherwise the first request pays 6–7 s.
- **Outcome claims lean on operative provisions.** Retrieval ranks paragraphs by topic, so the sentence stating the result is often missing from the top hits; adding the operative provisions fixed a first demo run where 4 of 6 claims were rejected as unsupported. Legal summaries and legislation have no operative provisions, so claims about their outcomes must come from the retrieved text.
- **Model parameters differ per model.** For example `temperature` is not accepted at all, and turning thinking off needs `"disabled"` on Haiku but `"between_tools"` on Sonnet. `llm.py` handles both and falls back on the API's error message, but a new model may need another tweak.
